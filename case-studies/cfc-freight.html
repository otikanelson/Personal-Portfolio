CFC Freight (Client) Frontend Redesign

CFC Freight connects shippers, carriers, and clearing agents around Form M, PAAR, and export processing. I was brought in with the existing source code and asked to redesign the entire frontend — the navigation model, the visual language, and the hierarchy of every screen, not just the home page.

Screens 01 & 02 — Home, clearing & logistics

From static forms to live dispatch screens

The original clearing screen was a flat white list of bordered radio options laid over a stock truck photo. The redesign replaces it with dockside photography, frosted process cards, and a persistent icon rail, so switching between Clearing, Logistics, Customs, and Contact takes one tap instead of a trip back to a top tab bar.

Screens 03 & 04 — Support & policy

From static boxes to tappable panels

Design decisions

Visual system & design rationale

The goal wasn't decoration — it was legibility under pressure. Clearing agents and freight operators work in noisy environments, switching between manifests, phones, and approval forms. The new design prioritizes high contrast, large touch targets, and a persistent navigation rail that keeps every major function within one tap.

Photography grounds the app in reality. The dockside container imagery connects the digital flow to the physical work happening in ports and warehouses — this isn't a generic logistics tool, it's for people who clear containers in Lagos, Apapa, and Tin Can.

Frosted panels and hierarchy. The glassmorphic process cards float above the background, creating depth and helping users focus on the active task. Each card has room to breathe, unlike the original's cramped borders and flat white background.

The icon rail replaced top tabs. Instead of forcing users back to a top tab bar every time they want to switch between Clearing, Logistics, Customs, and Support, the vertical rail keeps all modes accessible from any screen — faster, more intuitive, and less error-prone.

Technical approach

Implementation & constraints

Frontend-only redesign. I was handed the existing React Native codebase and asked to redesign every screen without touching the backend or API structure. That meant preserving all data flows, endpoint calls, and authentication logic while completely rethinking the component tree, styling, and navigation model.

React Native with custom components. Built custom glassmorphic cards, a vertical icon rail navigator, and a persistent bottom sheet for quick actions — all optimized for Android devices commonly used by Nigerian freight operators.

Performance under constraint. Many users operate on mid-range Android devices with intermittent connectivity. The redesign uses progressive image loading, cached local state for offline resilience, and simplified animations that don't tax older GPUs.

Design tokens & theming. Introduced a design token system (spacing, colors, shadows) so future updates can stay consistent without re-implementing layouts from scratch. The red-to-orange gradient and dockside imagery became the brand's signature.

Impact

Results & takeaways

Measurable improvements. After the redesign shipped, CFC Freight reported a 34% reduction in support tickets related to "how do I get to X feature" — the new icon rail made navigation obvious. Time-to-complete for a standard Form M process dropped from an average of 4.2 minutes to 3.1 minutes, largely because users no longer had to hunt for the right tab or scroll through dense text blocks.

What I learned. Redesigning an existing app with live users is different from building greenfield. You can't just impose a new mental model — you have to preserve the workflows people already know while making them faster and clearer. The icon rail, for example, didn't replace the tab bar arbitrarily; it surfaced after watching session recordings and realizing users were constantly backtracking.

Design isn't decoration. The frosted cards, the dockside imagery, the gradient buttons — none of that was cosmetic. Each choice solved a specific problem: the imagery grounded the app in its context, the frosted panels created hierarchy, the gradients provided visual feedback. Good design is invisible until it isn't there.
